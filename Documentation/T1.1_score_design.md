## Objective

Define the two things Phase 2 cannot start without: **what the hallucination score is**, and **what experiment would falsify the claim**. Nothing is calibrated in this phase and no guarantee is proved — the output is a written, reference-implemented score plus a protocol and ablation matrix that the empirical track can execute against.

**Why the score comes before the calibration:** conformal calibration is score-agnostic — it will happily produce a valid flag from a useless score, and the result would be a technically correct paper with no detection power. The score is where the operator knowledge actually enters (D2), and it is the only part of the pipeline that is specific to this problem rather than borrowed from the conformal literature.

---

## Design principle — the score lives in the null space (D2)

Because A is known, every reconstruction splits into a range component (pinned by the data) and a null-space component (invisible to the operator). Hallucination can *only* live in the latter. The score therefore emphasises structure in the free component that **also disagrees across independent solver draws**, combined with evidence from the measurement residual:

- **null-space energy** — is there structure here the data never constrained?
- **cross-sample disagreement** — do independent draws from the prior disagree about it? (Structure that is stable across draws is prior-consistent; structure that flips between draws is invented.)
- **measurement residual** — does the reconstruction actually explain the data it was given?

The three components are combined into one scalar per region. The ablation matrix must isolate each, because a reviewer will ask which one is doing the work — and it is entirely possible that disagreement alone is competitive, which would be an honest and publishable negative finding about the operator decomposition.

## Design principle — no calibration claims in this phase

Phase 1 produces scores, not flags. The temptation is to wire up split conformal early because it is easy and produces a satisfying coverage number; the risk is that the number is computed under a fixed operator, looks fine, and quietly establishes the wrong baseline in everyone's head. The naive-split-conformal baseline is a **Phase 2** artefact, built specifically to be shown failing under shift (see `01_track2_data_operator_pipeline.md`, WI-5).

---

## Metrics

Phase 1 has no coverage numbers. What it must produce instead:

- **Score sanity:** does the score rank known-hallucinated regions above clean ones on a small hand-checked set? A score that fails this cannot be rescued by calibration.
- **Component separation:** null-space energy, cross-sample disagreement, and residual are not measuring the same thing — show their correlation is low enough that combining them is justified.
- **Cost model:** measured seconds per solver draw → the Phase 2/3 compute budget, with a go/no-go on sample count.
